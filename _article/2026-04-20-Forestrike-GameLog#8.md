# In Case You Don’t Know What a Roguelike Is

At some point, more and more developers started making roguelike games.

For those unfamiliar with the genre, roguelikes usually have two defining characteristics:

- Procedurally generated levels
- Permanent death

[![](https://shared.fastly.steamstatic.com/store_item_assets/steam/apps/239350/ss_cd898bb5ce94c255d7e3ef17caecc157f6af535b.1920x1080.jpg?t=1632912566)](https://store.steampowered.com/app/239350/Spelunky/)
{: .text-center}

Procedural generation ensures that each run feels unique, while permanent death forces players to learn from failure rather than memorize layouts. That’s exactly what makes roguelikes fun. You learn the game, not the map, and keep improving until you succeed. 

I’m a big roguelike/roguelite fan myself and have played nearly a hundred games in the genre.

However, should you make your game a roguelike?

**DON’T.**

# Forestrike Is a Roguelike

[![](https://shared.fastly.steamstatic.com/store_item_assets/steam/apps/2325920/ss_aec1ea6ab6ac22381054e61569d88d5214e453ac.1920x1080.jpg?t=1773936700)](https://store.steampowered.com/app/2325920/Forestrike/)
{: .text-center}

Forestrike is a game where you play as a martial artist named Yu, who embarks on a journey to free the Emperor from an evil Admiral’s influence. Yu possesses a mysterious technique called “Foresight,” which allows him to rehearse encounters in his mind. During each fight, you can practice as many times as you want before committing to the real battle.

[![](https://shared.fastly.steamstatic.com/store_item_assets/steam/apps/2325920/ss_4181b04fc4c92f7606eb13d24937f074f49cea6f.1920x1080.jpg?t=1773936700)](https://store.steampowered.com/app/2325920/Forestrike/)
{: .text-center}

The one aspect where this game benefits from being a roguelike is its story. No matter whether you win or lose, you always return to the beginning. Every run is essentially a mental rehearsal using “Foresight.” In reality, Yu never actually embarks on the journey. This is a brilliant narrative device. It neatly integrates roguelike elements into the story while also adding a deeper metaphor.

But despite that, I find the game not fun largely because it is a roguelike.

# Forestrike Doesn’t Need to Be a Roguelike

Beyond its narrative justification, the game doesn’t actually benefit from being a roguelike. Each run lets players learn martial arts randomly, and success depends on building strong synergies. At the same time, combat requires precise execution and mastery of enemy patterns, which you practice through “Foresight.”

However, both the strategic and action aspects feel frustrating.

[![](https://shared.fastly.steamstatic.com/store_item_assets/steam/apps/2325920/ss_5596e91f367a22c248dba99b7c8ef05d92e9a436.1920x1080.jpg?t=1773936700)](https://store.steampowered.com/app/2325920/Forestrike/)
{: .text-center}

On the strategy side, synergies are unclear, and some martial arts are overwhelmingly stronger than others. Getting these dominant abilities often determines whether you can win. Some encounters deal unavoidable damage unless your build is strong enough, which undermines player skill. On top of that, the game offers limited meaningful choices, these options usually just feel useless.

[![](https://shared.fastly.steamstatic.com/store_item_assets/steam/apps/2325920/ss_b9ac9b9360ccd0009391fa3700505b593cc8f45e.1920x1080.jpg?t=1773936700)](https://store.steampowered.com/app/2325920/Forestrike/)
{: .text-center}

On the action side, even with unlimited practice, you’ll still take damage due to tight input windows. Making mistakes in a game is fine, but what matters is what you learn from them. Here, failures often come from tiny execution errors rather than new mechanics or patterns. As a result, there’s little meaningful learning. The core “Foresight” mechanic actually undermines the roguelike structure. Instead of turning failure into growth, it turns failure into frustration. Learning that you simply need to “be more careful next time” everytime isn’t engaging.

In my view, Forestrike is a strong example of a game that shouldn’t be a roguelike but was made into one anyway. Its core mechanic could have been executed better in a more structured, hand-crafted experience.

# Should You Make a Roguelike Game?

Many developers misunderstand roguelikes. They assume that procedural generation and permadeath mean less work. You won't need to design levels by hand, and infinite replayability comes for free.

Less work, infinite gameplay. Sounds great, right?

**Wrong.**

Procedural generation means you must ensure your algorithm consistently produces levels with the same quality as handcrafted ones. How does it sounds easy? Permanent death means players will experience frustration when they fail. To make that frustration meaningful, you must clearly communicate why they failed so they can learn. Designing good levels by hand is already hard, and what makes you think your algorithm can do it better? It often takes far more time than expected, even when you already expect it to take a lot of time.

There are too many roguelike and roguelite games today, and many feel like they adopted the structure without a good reason. Forestrike is one example.

[![](https://shared.fastly.steamstatic.com/store_item_assets/steam/apps/2132850/ss_91952981162e91bb26079bef1291394740e5fe70.1920x1080.jpg?t=1771398437)](https://store.steampowered.com/app/2132850/Rabbit_and_Steel/)
{: .text-center}

Another is Rabbit & Steel. The fun of MMO-style raiding comes from learning and mastering mechanics and boss patterns. Winning because someone in the team have a super broken build undermines that experience. To be fair, Rabbit & Steel is reasonably well-balanced, so it’s not a bad game. But still, the roguelike elements still feel somewhat unnecessary.

[![](https://shared.fastly.steamstatic.com/store_item_assets/steam/apps/1839810/8c21046a189054ce04c26fbefa91508771ab69bd/ss_8c21046a189054ce04c26fbefa91508771ab69bd.1920x1080.jpg?t=1775711961)](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1839810/Out_Of_Hands/)
{: .text-center}

On the other hand, some games benefit from not forcing roguelike elements. Out of Hands works well as a structured card game, and it also tells a completed a awesome story. Even though it later added a roguelike mode, it’s not nearly as enjoyable and I’m glad it’s not the main focus.

[![](https://shared.fastly.steamstatic.com/store_item_assets/steam/apps/1569580/ss_d04f2e766f538d3680b28574ec97f06b3274aafc.1920x1080.jpg?t=1776265019)](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1569580/Blue_Prince/)
{: .text-center}

Still, I am not againsting the idea of making a roguelike. That said, roguelike elements can work when they truly support the core design. Blue Prince is a great example of combining roguelike structure with puzzle game. It justifies its format through both gameplay and narrative, and it clearly went through extensive playtesting to achieve that balance.

# Final Thought

Before deciding to make a roguelike, ask yourself:

**Is it because I want to make a roguelike, or the game needs to be a roguelike?**
